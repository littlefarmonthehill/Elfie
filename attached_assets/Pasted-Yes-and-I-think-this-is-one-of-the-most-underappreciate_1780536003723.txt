Yes, and I think this is one of the most underappreciated impacts of AI on software.

Historically, software companies had to think:

“Will enough customers use this feature to justify the cost of building and maintaining it?”

If only 5 customers wanted it, the answer was usually no.

The costs were:

* Product managers
* Developers
* QA
* Documentation
* Support
* Future maintenance

So software gravitated toward the “average customer.”

⸻

With AI development agents, the economics start to change.

Imagine Elfie has 500 LEGO sellers.

Today:

* 400 sellers want inventory management.
* 300 want repricing.
* 250 want order management.

Those become core features.

But maybe:

* One seller specializes in retired Star Wars sets.
* One seller runs convention events.
* One seller rents LEGO rather than selling it.
* One seller wants a custom profitability model.

Traditionally those niche requests get rejected.

With AI, you could potentially say:

“Generate a custom module for this customer.”

The development cost might drop from weeks to hours.

⸻

The really interesting shift is from:

One product → many customers

to

One platform → many customized experiences

Think of Elfie as providing:

* Authentication
* Data storage
* Inventory engine
* Marketplace connections
* Billing

Then AI generates:

* Custom dashboards
* Custom reports
* Custom workflows
* Custom automations
* Custom business rules

for individual customers.

⸻

This is similar to what happened with websites.

In the 1990s, every website was custom-built.

Then platforms emerged:

* WordPress
* Shopify
* Wix

The platform provided the foundation, and customization happened on top.

AI may do the same thing for business software.

⸻

For PlanetBrick and Elfie specifically, I suspect your long-term moat may not be the features themselves.

A bigger moat could be:

* Deep LEGO-specific data.
* Knowledge of LEGO sellers’ workflows.
* Connections to LEGO marketplaces.
* Historical sales data.
* Community knowledge.

If that’s true, then AI makes custom features easier, not harder.

The question changes from:

“Can we afford to build this feature?”

to:

“Does this feature fit within our LEGO platform and data model?”

⸻

One caution: custom features can create maintenance chaos if each customer gets a completely unique codebase.

The likely winning model is:

Shared core platform
+
AI-generated extensions/plugins
+
Strong underlying data model

That gives customers the feeling of bespoke software while keeping the platform manageable.

Ironically, AI may bring us back to something that existed decades ago: software tailored to individual organizations. The difference is that instead of needing a team of consultants and developers, an AI agent can create and maintain much of that customization at a fraction of the cost. For a niche market like LEGO sellers, that could be a significant competitive advantage.
